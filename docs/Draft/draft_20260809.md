**Anti-statist sustainability backlash: Libertarian narratives and the erosion of state capacities in Argentina under Milei.**

**1\. Introduction** 

**2\. Theoretical Background** 

**2.1 Climate obstructionism and sustainability-policy backlash**

Political opposition to climate and sustainability policies has attracted growing scholarly attention over the past two decades. Early research focused primarily on climate denial, examining how fossil-fuel interests, conservative think tanks, contrarian scientists, and sympathetic media organizations sought to challenge the scientific consensus on anthropogenic climate change and resist regulatory intervention (McCright & Dunlap, 2000; Jacques, Dunlap, & Freeman, 2008; Oreskes & Conway, 2010; Brulle, 2014). Rather than simply producing alternative scientific claims, these actors worked to maintain political uncertainty long after scientific consensus had emerged, thereby delaying regulatory responses and preserving existing economic interests (Dunlap & McCright, 2015; Brulle, 2021).

As the scientific consensus became increasingly difficult to contest, however, political opposition evolved beyond explicit denial. Instead of rejecting climate science outright, many actors shifted their attention towards questioning the urgency, effectiveness, fairness, or economic desirability of climate policies. Lamb et al. (2020) conceptualize these narratives as discourses of climate delay, identifying a range of rhetorical strategies that acknowledge climate change while discouraging transformative action. These include emphasizing the economic costs of mitigation, promoting incremental or insufficient technological solutions, shifting responsibility onto other actors or countries, and portraying ambitious climate action as unrealistic or socially unacceptable. Climate obstruction therefore increasingly operates through the contestation of policy responses rather than the rejection of climate science itself (Lamb et al., 2020; Stoddard et al., 2021).

More recently, scholars have argued that opposition extends beyond individual climate policies to encompass broader resistance to sustainability transitions. Research on climate backlash, green backlash, anti-environmental countermovements, and resistance to net-zero transitions emphasizes that sustainability policies have become embedded within wider political conflicts over economic redistribution, state intervention, cultural identity, national sovereignty, and the authority of scientific expertise (Lockwood, 2018; Forchtner, 2019; Colgan, Green, & Hale, 2021; Bosetti, 2026). Rather than opposing only specific environmental measures, political actors increasingly challenge the broader societal transformations associated with decarbonization, environmental protection, and sustainability governance. As Bosetti (2026) argues, contemporary green backlash should be understood as a multidimensional political phenomenon in which environmental policies become symbolic targets within wider ideological conflicts over the role of the state, markets, expertise, and social change.

This broader perspective has also expanded the understanding of climate obstruction. Rather than referring solely to organized climate denial, obstruction is increasingly conceptualized as a repertoire of discursive and institutional practices aimed at delaying, weakening, or delegitimizing climate and sustainability governance (Brulle, 2021; Stoddard et al., 2021). These practices may include attacks on scientific expertise, regulatory rollback, reductions in environmental funding, administrative weakening, or the framing of sustainability policies as economically harmful, ideologically motivated, or democratically illegitimate. Climate obstruction thus concerns not only whether governments adopt environmental policies, but also whether they sustain the institutional conditions necessary for their implementation.

Despite these important advances, three limitations remain. First, much of the literature continues to focus on climate policy rather than the broader governance structures underpinning sustainability transitions. Second, empirical attention has concentrated primarily on right-wing populist or conservative opposition rooted in distributive conflicts, sectoral interests, or nationalist politics, particularly in Europe and North America (Lockwood, 2018; Forchtner, 2019). Comparatively less attention has been devoted to cases from the Global South and to political projects whose primary objective is not merely to oppose environmental regulation but to fundamentally redefine the role of the state. Finally, while obstruction and backlash research has documented how political actors contest the legitimacy of sustainability policies, it has devoted comparatively little attention to how these narratives translate into the weakening of the institutional and organizational capacities required for sustainability governance.

These limitations are particularly relevant for understanding the Argentine case under Javier Milei. Rather than opposing only particular environmental or climate policies, the Milei administration appears to articulate a broader critique of public intervention, scientific expertise, and state coordination. To understand this distinctive configuration, it is therefore necessary to complement the obstruction and backlash literature with scholarship on libertarian populism, anti-statist governance, policy dismantling, and state capacity.

## **2.2 Libertarian populism and anti-statist governance**

Although the literature on climate obstruction and sustainability-policy backlash explains why climate and environmental policies become politically contested, it provides only a partial explanation for the Argentine case. Under the Milei administration, environmental policy is challenged as part of a broader project of state retrenchment rather than as an isolated policy domain. Understanding this distinctive configuration therefore requires engaging with scholarship on populism, libertarianism, and anti-statist governance.

Research on right-wing populism has consistently shown that environmental and climate policies are frequently framed as products of detached political elites, technocratic governance, and international organizations that undermine national sovereignty and individual freedoms (Mudde, 2007; Müller, 2016; Lockwood, 2018; Forchtner, 2019; Norris & Inglehart, 2019). Climate policy thus becomes embedded within broader conflicts over representation, expertise, and the legitimacy of liberal democratic institutions. In Europe and North America, opposition to environmental regulation has often combined anti-elitism, distrust of scientific expertise, nationalism, and resistance to supranational governance (Forchtner & Kølvraa, 2015; Kulin et al., 2021; Küppers, 2022). Similar dynamics have increasingly been identified in the Global South, where environmental governance has become intertwined with debates over sovereignty, development, and resource nationalism. The Bolsonaro administration in Brazil, for example, combined environmental deregulation with attacks on environmental agencies, scientific institutions, and international environmental governance, while justifying these reforms through narratives of national development, territorial sovereignty, and resistance to foreign interference (Hochstetler, 2021; Pereira & Viola, 2022; Viola & Gonçalves, 2022).

The Argentine case shares several characteristics with these broader populist dynamics, including anti-elitism, criticism of international organizations, and skepticism towards environmental regulation. However, the ideological foundations of the Milei administration differ in important respects. Rather than primarily advancing a nationalist or productivist agenda, Milei explicitly presents his government as the world's first genuinely libertarian administration (Merke, 2026). While empirical studies of libertarian governments remain scarce, the libertarian intellectual tradition has long emphasized individual liberty, market coordination, limited government, and skepticism toward state intervention. Hayek's critique of central planning argued that knowledge is inherently dispersed throughout society and that governments cannot effectively coordinate economic activity through centralized decision-making (Hayek, 1945, 1960). Later neoliberal scholarship similarly challenged the expansion of state regulation and highlighted the coordinating role of competitive markets (Harvey, 2005; Mirowski, 2013; Plehwe, 2014). Although these traditions do not reject scientific knowledge per se, they question the capacity and legitimacy of governments to organize society through planning, regulation, and expert administration.

Recent scholarship has further suggested that contemporary populist movements increasingly challenge not only specific public policies but also the institutions responsible for producing authoritative knowledge and informing public decision-making (Jasanoff, 2004, 2011; Fischer, 2009; Weingart, 1999). Public universities, scientific agencies, expert bureaucracies, and regulatory institutions become associated with technocratic elites whose authority is portrayed as politically biased, ideologically captured, or disconnected from ordinary citizens. In this context, opposition extends beyond individual policies towards broader challenges to the legitimacy of public expertise and state-coordinated governance. The Milei administration appears to deepen this tendency (Merke, 2026).

This discussion suggests that libertarian populism may provide a distinctive ideological foundation for contesting sustainability governance. Rather than focusing exclusively on environmental policy, it raises broader questions about the legitimacy of state intervention, public expertise, and regulatory governance. However, while the literature helps explain these ideological foundations, it provides less insight into the institutional mechanisms through which such ideological projects reshape the state itself. The following section therefore examines the literature on policy dismantling to understand how political ideas are translated into institutional change.

**2.3 Policy dismantling: from ideological projects to institutional change**

While the literature on sustainability-policy backlash explains why governments may oppose climate and environmental policies, it pays comparatively less attention to how these political projects are translated into institutional change. This question has been extensively examined within the literature on policy dismantling, which studies the reduction, removal, or weakening of existing public policies and institutions (Bauer et al., 2012; Bauer & Knill, 2014).

Policy dismantling is commonly defined as a deliberate process through which governments reduce the density, intensity, or scope of public policies, either by removing policy instruments, reducing resources, weakening implementation, or reorganizing the institutions responsible for policy delivery (Bauer et al., 2012). Importantly, dismantling does not necessarily imply the complete abolition of policies. Governments may instead pursue more gradual or less visible strategies, including budget reductions, organizational restructuring, administrative simplification, deregulation, or the weakening of implementation and enforcement capacities (Green-Pedersen, 2002; Jordan, Bauer, & Green-Pedersen, 2013). Consequently, policy change often occurs through cumulative institutional adjustments rather than through highly visible legislative reforms.

The dismantling literature has also highlighted the political constraints associated with retrenchment. Because removing public policies often generates electoral costs and opposition from affected stakeholders, governments frequently adopt strategies that minimize political conflict, including incremental reforms, organizational mergers, delegation, or reductions in administrative capacity rather than outright policy repeal (Green-Pedersen, 2002; Bauer et al., 2012). Such approaches allow governments to reshape the functioning of the state while maintaining formal policy commitments or avoiding direct political confrontation.

Recent institutional scholarship further suggests that significant policy change frequently occurs through gradual transformations rather than abrupt institutional rupture (Mahoney & Thelen, 2010; Streeck & Thelen, 2005). Institutional conversion, displacement, layering, or drift may progressively alter the objectives and functioning of public organizations without requiring comprehensive legislative reform. These perspectives broaden the understanding of dismantling beyond the removal of individual policy instruments and highlight the importance of administrative and organizational change in explaining long-term institutional transformation.

Although this literature provides important insights into the mechanisms through which governments retrench public intervention, its primary focus has remained on policy outputs and institutional reform. Comparatively less attention has been devoted to the implications of dismantling for the broader capacities of the state to govern complex societal transitions. This distinction is particularly relevant in the field of sustainability, where effective governance depends not only on the existence of environmental policies but also on the fiscal resources, administrative capabilities, regulatory institutions, scientific expertise, and coordination mechanisms required to design, implement, monitor, and adapt those policies over time. Understanding the consequences of policy dismantling therefore requires complementing this literature with scholarship on policy and state capacities.

**2.4 State capacities for sustainability governance**

Policy dismantling concerns not only the reduction of existing policy instruments but also the weakening of the institutional resources through which governments formulate and pursue collective objectives. This broader consequence can be examined through the literature on state and policy capacity. While state capacity generally refers to the ability of public authorities to formulate objectives and implement them effectively, policy capacity focuses more specifically on the competencies and resources required to perform policy functions, from producing knowledge and designing interventions to coordinating actors and enforcing decisions. The two concepts overlap, but policy capacity directs attention to the concrete organizational foundations of government action rather than treating the state as a unitary actor.

A particularly influential framework defines policy capacity as the combination of skills and resources required for effective policymaking and distinguishes among analytical, operational, and political capacities operating at the individual, organizational, and systemic levels (Wu et al., 2015). Analytical capacity concerns the ability to generate, interpret, and apply scientific and technical knowledge, evaluate policy alternatives, and support evidence-informed decision-making. Operational capacity refers to the organizational resources needed to design, coordinate, administer, monitor, and enforce public policies. Political capacity encompasses the ability to build legitimacy, coordinate stakeholders, manage conflict, and sustain political support for collective action. These dimensions are mutually reinforcing: analytical knowledge has limited influence without organizations capable of acting upon it, while administrative resources are unlikely to be effective without the political capacity to mobilize support and coordinate across institutions.

This perspective is particularly relevant for sustainability governance. Environmental and climate challenges are characterized by long-term time horizons, scientific uncertainty, cross-sectoral interdependencies, and conflicts over the distribution of costs and benefits. As a result, governing sustainability requires considerably more than adopting individual regulations or setting formal policy targets. It depends on the state's ability to produce and mobilize scientific knowledge, monitor environmental change, formulate long-term strategies, coordinate across ministries and levels of government, engage societal actors, enforce regulatory standards, and adapt policies over time (Lemos & Agrawal, 2006). Research on adaptive climate governance similarly emphasizes that responding to complex environmental problems requires the simultaneous development of analytical, administrative, and political capacities rather than reliance on any single governance instrument (Jordan & Huitema, 2014).

The capacity perspective therefore provides an important complement to the policy dismantling literature. A policy may formally remain in force while the state's ability to implement it gradually deteriorates. Budget reductions can weaken monitoring systems, scientific research, inspections, and programme delivery; organizational restructuring may fragment responsibilities and erode institutional memory; and the downgrading or elimination of specialized agencies can undermine coordination and technical expertise. Likewise, weakening public research institutions or scientific advisory bodies reduces the knowledge infrastructure upon which evidence-based policymaking depends (Jasanoff, 2004). In such contexts, governments may preserve formal policy commitments while simultaneously diminishing the institutional capacities necessary to achieve them.

Capacity erosion should therefore not be understood merely as the unintended consequence of fiscal constraints or administrative inefficiency. Rather, it may result from deliberate political choices regarding which functions the state should perform, which forms of expertise are regarded as legitimate, and which public institutions should retain authority and resources. This understanding is consistent with broader scholarship on the selective restructuring of the state, which emphasizes that governments often strengthen certain capacities while intentionally reducing others in accordance with changing political and ideological priorities (Jessop, 2002). This observation directly connects the capacity literature with the ideological dynamics discussed in the preceding sections. When public intervention, environmental regulation, or scientific expertise are portrayed as inherently inefficient, coercive, or illegitimate, reducing the capacities that sustain them may be presented not as a governance failure but as a political achievement. From this perspective, the weakening of state capacities can constitute an intended outcome of an anti-statist governing project rather than an unintended by-product of fiscal adjustment.

Taken together, these four strands of literature provide important but partial explanations of the contemporary transformation of sustainability governance. Research on sustainability backlash explains the growing political resistance to climate and environmental policies, while studies of libertarian populism illuminate the ideological narratives through which state intervention and scientific expertise are challenged. The literature on policy dismantling identifies the institutional mechanisms through which governments reshape public policies, and the state capacity perspective highlights the broader governance consequences of these reforms. However, these perspectives have largely evolved in parallel, offering limited guidance on how ideological narratives become translated into institutional change. To address this gap, the following section develops an analytical framework that conceptualizes anti-statist sustainability backlash as a dual process combining discursive delegitimization and institutional erosion.

**3\. An Analytical Framework for Anti-statist Sustainability Backlash**

**3.1 Anti-statist sustainability backlash: a dual-process framework**

Building on the preceding discussion, we conceptualize anti-statist sustainability backlash as a dual process through which libertarian ideas are translated into transformations of sustainability governance. Rather than understanding backlash solely as opposition to particular environmental policies, we argue that anti-statist sustainability backlash simultaneously seeks to undermine the legitimacy of sustainability governance and to weaken the institutional capacities through which governments pursue sustainability objectives.

The framework distinguishes between two analytically distinct but mutually reinforcing dimensions: discursive delegitimization and institutional erosion**.** The first captures the narratives through which sustainability governance is portrayed as politically, scientifically, or culturally illegitimate. The second captures the institutional changes that reduce the state's capacity to formulate, coordinate, implement, monitor, and adapt sustainability policies. Together, these dimensions explain how ideological opposition to sustainability can become embedded in governmental institutions and administrative practice.

While analytically distinct, the two dimensions are closely interconnected. Discursive delegitimization provides the political and ideological justification for institutional reforms, whereas institutional erosion reduces the state's ability to sustain and defend public action on sustainability over time. This interaction creates a self-reinforcing dynamic in which weakening institutions further diminish the capacity to generate scientific knowledge, coordinate public policies, and maintain long-term sustainability governance. The following sections elaborate each dimension in greater detail.

**3.2 Discursive delegitimization**

The first dimension captures how governmental discourse challenges the legitimacy of sustainability governance by questioning its institutional, epistemic, and normative foundations. Rather than focusing on disagreement over specific environmental measures, this dimension examines broader narratives that redefine the role of the state, the authority of scientific expertise, and the societal legitimacy of sustainability itself. These narratives create a political environment in which institutional reforms can be justified as restoring freedom, reducing bureaucracy, or eliminating ideological bias. Three interrelated forms of discursive delegitimization are distinguished.

The first concerns the delegitimization of the state's role in sustainability governance. Here, public intervention is portrayed as inefficient, coercive, or incompatible with individual liberty and market competition. Environmental regulation, state planning, and multilateral environmental cooperation are framed not as legitimate instruments for addressing collective problems but as unjustified constraints on economic freedom and private initiative. The object of contestation is therefore not only individual policies but the broader legitimacy of state involvement in sustainability governance.

The second concerns the delegitimization of public science and expertise. Sustainability governance relies heavily on scientific knowledge, technical expertise, and specialized public institutions capable of informing policymaking under conditions of uncertainty. Anti-statist discourse frequently challenges these institutions by portraying scientists, universities, public research organizations, and technical experts as politically biased, ideologically captured, or disconnected from society. Such narratives weaken the authority of expert knowledge as a legitimate basis for collective decision-making and contribute to the erosion of trust in public institutions responsible for producing and mobilizing scientific evidence.

The third concerns the cultural delegitimization of sustainability. Rather than treating climate and environmental policies as responses to ecological challenges, sustainability is embedded within broader ideological conflicts surrounding culture, identity, and political values. Environmental governance is associated with wider narratives concerning "wokeism," globalism, socialism, or progressive cultural agendas, transforming sustainability from a policy domain into a symbol of broader political confrontation. This cultural reframing broadens opposition beyond specific environmental policies and positions sustainability governance as part of a wider ideological project.

Taken together, these three forms of delegitimization target the institutional, epistemic, and normative pillars upon which sustainability governance is built. Rather than representing independent narratives, they collectively contribute to redefining sustainability governance as politically illegitimate and therefore as an appropriate object of institutional reform.

**3.3 Institutional erosion**

The second dimension focuses on the institutional consequences of anti-statist sustainability backlash. Building on the literature on policy dismantling and state capacities, we define institutional erosion as the progressive weakening of the capacities that enable governments to formulate, coordinate, implement, enforce, monitor, and adapt sustainability policies. The emphasis is therefore not solely on whether particular regulations are formally abolished, but on whether the institutional infrastructure required for effective sustainability governance is preserved or weakened.

For the purposes of this article, state capacities for sustainability governance refer to the institutional, financial, administrative, regulatory, coordinative, and knowledge-based resources that enable public authorities to pursue long-term sustainability objectives. This conception extends beyond the implementation of individual policies to encompass the broader organizational and epistemic foundations that make sustained public action possible.

Institutional erosion may occur through several interconnected mechanisms. Budgetary retrenchment reduces the financial and human resources available to organizations responsible for sustainability governance, limiting their ability to perform regulatory, scientific, and administrative functions. Institutional downgrading weakens the authority, autonomy, specialization, or coordinating role of ministries, agencies, and public research institutions responsible for sustainability governance. Finally, policy dismantling reduces or removes regulations, programmes, planning instruments, and implementation arrangements that support sustainability objectives. Although analytically distinguishable, these mechanisms frequently reinforce one another. Reduced budgets weaken organizational performance; institutional restructuring fragments responsibilities and erodes institutional memory; and the dismantling of policies further diminishes the political justification for maintaining specialized governance capacities.

The framework does not assume that anti-statist governments seek to reduce state capacity in general. Rather, it examines the selective erosion of those state capacities associated with sustainability governance, while other governmental functions may be maintained or even strengthened. This distinction is particularly important for understanding contemporary anti-statist projects, which often combine a reduced role for the regulatory and environmental state with the reinforcement of other state functions.

**4\. Empirical Research Design** 

This article adopts an explanatory qualitative case-study design to examine how anti-statist sustainability backlash becomes translated into institutional change. Argentina under Javier Milei provides a theoretically relevant case because the government has explicitly challenged the legitimacy of environmental governance, scientific institutions, and state intervention while simultaneously implementing extensive institutional reforms. Rather than aiming for statistical generalization, the study seeks analytical generalization by refining an emerging conceptual framework.

Consistent with the dual-process framework developed in Section 3, the empirical strategy triangulates two independently analyzed data sources. Corpus A — the full population of Javier Milei's presidential speeches and public discourses — is analyzed through abductive qualitative content analysis to trace discursive delegitimization (Dimension 1). Corpus B — formal governmental documentation (executive decrees, budgets, and organizational records) — is analyzed through structured documentary analysis to trace institutional erosion (Dimension 2). This section reports the design, collection, and coding procedures for Corpus A, on which the article's empirical findings are based, and specifies the design for the Corpus B documentary analysis, which constitutes the next stage of the broader research program.

**4.1 Data collection**

*4.1.1 Corpus A: presidential speeches and public discourse*

Corpus A comprises the full population — not a sample — of Javier Milei's identifiable speeches, interviews, and public discourses as a presidential candidate and as President of Argentina, rather than a purposive or climate-filtered subset. The corpus was assembled and is maintained in a master tracking database (193 entries as of this writing) recording, for each event, its date, venue, country, event type, a climate-relevance heuristic, and the source URL. Full verbatim transcripts were collected primarily from Casa Rosada's official "Discursos" registry, supplemented by YouTube transcripts and third-party institutional transcripts (e.g., the World Economic Forum, the Council on Foreign Relations) for events not transcribed by the official registry, most notably the pre-inauguration second presidential debate of October 2023. Each transcript was saved as a structured text file preserving frontmatter metadata (title, date, source URL, source type) and the full, unedited body text; every source URL is logged in a collection registry, and inaccessible sources were flagged and excluded rather than silently dropped. Speeches were preserved in their original language (predominantly Spanish, with a subset of internationally hosted addresses transcribed in English by their host institution); no primary source was translated prior to coding.

The final corpus totals 193 speech events (10 December 2023 – 29 April 2026, plus the 8 October 2023 pre-inauguration debate) spanning 23 event-type categories — state-of-the-nation addresses ("Apertura de Sesiones Ordinarias"), international forums (World Economic Forum, G20, UN General Assembly), cadenas nacionales, business chambers and stock exchanges, universities and think tanks, and bilateral and ceremonial appearances — and 14 countries; two-thirds of the corpus (66.8%) was delivered domestically, with the United States, Israel, and Spain accounting for the largest foreign-venue concentrations. **4.2 Coding procedure for Corpus A: abductive qualitative content analysis**

*Analytical approach.* The analysis of Corpus A follows an abductive coding strategy, iterating between theory and data rather than applying a fixed deductive scheme or building categories from the ground up (Timmermans & Tavory, 2012). A first set of deductive "seed" categories was derived from the literature on climate-policy backlash, populism, policy dismantling, and epistemic governance discussed in Section 2; the coding process then remained open to refining, merging, or adding categories inductively where the data revealed patterns the seed categories did not capture (Fereday & Muir-Cochrane, 2006). The unit of analysis is the rhetorical passage — typically a paragraph — coded for the presence of one or more of the categories described below.

*Codebook development.* The coding scheme was developed and revised iteratively over four versions. An initial deductive codebook (v1) was extracted from the theoretical literature and the project's methodological references. This scheme was piloted on a benchmark subset of climate-salient flagship speeches (e.g., the WEF Davos addresses, the UN General Assembly addresses, and the Apertura de Sesiones Ordinarias) and revised twice (v2, v3) as coding proceeded. In June 2026, two members of the research team independently reviewed the full v3-coded benchmark corpus (98 code instances) and reconciled their assessments, a peer-debriefing step that produced the current codebook (v4): two redundant sub-codes were merged, one sub-code was renamed to better reflect its analytical function rather than Milei's own polemical vocabulary, one candidate inductive category was promoted to deductive status, two inductive categories were retired as either too rare or conceptually redundant with existing codes, and an explicit "overcoding rule" was formalized restricting each passage to one primary code unless a second code captures a genuinely distinct rhetorical move. This review also resolved a scope ambiguity — whether generalized anti-state, anti-regulatory, and anti-"casta" rhetoric should be coded only when explicitly climate-adjacent, or coded wherever it appears on the grounds that it constitutes the discursive infrastructure later applied to climate governance specifically. The team adopted the latter, broader reading (documented as "Option A"), consistent with the theoretical claim, developed in Section 3.2, that discursive delegitimization weakens the public and epistemic grounds for climate action "regardless of whether formal commitments remain in place." The finalized v4 codebook — three sub-dimensions comprising fourteen sub-codes, each with a formal definition and worked examples — was then applied to the full 193-speech corpus.

*Coding execution and disclosure of AI assistance.* Paragraph-level coding of the full corpus was performed by a large language model (Claude, Anthropic), operating under the fixed v4 codebook and an explicit annotation protocol specifying the unit of analysis, the one-primary-code-per-passage rule, and the inductive-category procedure. For each codeable passage, the model produced an inline code tag and a short analytic memo explaining the rhetorical move identified and why the chosen sub-code, rather than an adjacent one, applied; passages without codeable content were preserved verbatim and unmarked. This LLM-assisted procedure is disclosed here as a methodological choice, consistent with emerging norms for reporting AI-assisted qualitative coding (e.g., Alshami et al., 2025; Hitch, 2024). The research team retained control of the interpretive scheme throughout: humans authored and iteratively revised the codebook, defined the coding rules the model followed, and reviewed samples of the coded output; the model performed first-pass annotation at a scale (193 speeches) impractical to hand-code within the project timeline. No formal inter-rater reliability statistic (e.g., Cohen's kappa) was computed between human and model coding; this is noted as a limitation, and spot-validation of the coded corpus against source transcripts is recommended before submission (see Section 4.4, Methodological Notes).

*Quality control.* Two corpus-wide quality checks were built into the coding pass and are reported here for transparency. First, the frequency tally and all descriptive statistics are regenerated programmatically, directly from the coded files' code tags, rather than hand-maintained, eliminating drift between the coded corpus and reported counts (a hand-entry discrepancy was caught and corrected during development). Second, a full-text-integrity rule requires every paragraph of the source transcript — coded or not — to appear verbatim in the coded output; an earlier coding pass that had condensed long, repetitive passages was identified through internal audit and fully reconstructed from the raw transcripts before the corpus was finalized. Both checks function as an audit trail supporting the confirmability of the coded corpus (Tracy, 2010).

**4.4 Corpus overview**

**4.5 Analytical strategy**

The analysis proceeds in three stages. First, discursive delegitimization is examined through the coded Corpus A, reported in Section 5 (Findings): the distribution of codes across sub-dimensions, its evolution across the 2023–2026 period, and the passages that most directly bridge rhetoric and policy. Second, once collected, institutional reforms in Corpus B will be analyzed according to the three-dimensional operationalization of institutional erosion in Table 2. Third, both dimensions will be interpreted together to assess whether discursive narratives are accompanied by institutional changes that weaken state capacities for sustainability governance — anchored, in the first instance, on the November 2025 periglaciares announcement identified in the Corpus A analysis as the clearest documented case of rhetoric preceding a named policy mechanism. The objective is not to establish deterministic causality but to assess whether discursive delegitimization and institutional transformation constitute mutually reinforcing dimensions of sustainability-policy dismantling.
